Mexico-U.S. Trade Talks Move Forward, but Steel, Autos and Canada Complicate the Deal
After Canada suspended talks and the USMCA missed its July renewal, Mexico's economy chief is signaling progress from Milwaukee — but the hardest numbers are still on the table.

The Briefing:
At the G20 Trade Ministerial in Milwaukee, Economy Secretary Marcelo Ebrard said Mexico is "closer every day" to new agreements with Washington after a near-hour bilateral session with U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer on October 1.
Mexico's core unresolved demands: reducing the 25% tariff on cars and the 50% levy on steel and aluminum that have remained in place since the July 2025 trade arrangement.
With Canada's negotiations suspended since August 22, Mexico is the only party actively advancing formal USMCA review negotiations with the United States.
$30.76 billion in remittances reached Mexico in the first half of 2026 — a lifeline tethered to U.S. manufacturing employment, which depends on whether both governments can reach a durable trade framework.
Mexico's
Economy Secretary
Marcelo Ebrard
came out of the G20 Trade Ministerial in Milwaukee last week sounding the most optimistic he has in months. He had just spent nearly an hour across the table from
U.S.
Trade Representative
Jamieson Greer
, and his message was direct: "We are getting closer on many things — moving forward, narrowing positions, resolving issues. Every day we are closer to having new agreements,"
Ebrard told reporters from Milwaukee
.
The positive tone matters, but so does what remains unfinished. Tariffs of 25% on
Mexican
automobiles and 50% on steel and aluminum have not budged since the trade arrangement brokered in the summer of 2025. These are the levies that bite deepest into Mexico's two most critical export sectors — and the ones that most directly affect Latino workers in manufacturing belts from Michigan to Nuevo León. With U.S. midterm elections on November 3, the window to reach something meaningful is narrowing fast.

What Ebrard Said — and What He Left Open
The
Milwaukee ministerial ran September 30 to October 1, 2026
— a gathering of G20 trade ministers hosted by Ambassador Greer that also produced a joint statement on eliminating forced labor in global supply chains, signed by the trade ministers of the United States, Mexico, and Argentina. On the sidelines, Ebrard and Greer held their bilateral meeting, and Ebrard came out describing Mexico as "the country that exports the most and pays the least" among U.S. trading partners — a status he said he expected to protect.
He was careful, however, to acknowledge that the hardest items remain on the table. Tariffs on Mexican autos and on steel and aluminum exports — measures Ebrard described as falling under U.S. Section 232 and related executive authorities — have not moved despite the broader progress. Mexico has presented multiple proposals to Washington on both sectors and is awaiting a response, Ebrard said. Those two categories account for the bulk of what Mexico wants from any final framework.
The USMCA Review: A Missed Deadline and a New Track
The backdrop to these talks is significant. The USMCA included a mandatory joint review by July 1, 2026, at which point all three countries were to decide whether to extend the agreement for another 16 years. Washington chose
not to renew
the deal in its current form, triggering a period of rolling annual reviews that will last up to a decade unless a new agreement is locked in. The U.S. subsequently launched formal USMCA renegotiations with Mexico — but not with Canada — meaning that bilateral track is where the real action now sits.
That context explains the urgency Ebrard projects. A deal requiring annual renewal makes long-term investment bets in Mexico harder to justify for manufacturers. That uncertainty threatens the factory expansions and the stable payroll base that ultimately sustain the remittance economy. Ebrard's argument — repeated to trade ministers from Turkey, Saudi Arabia and elsewhere gathered in Milwaukee — is that Mexico holds a structurally superior position in the U.S. market compared to every other major exporter, and that a stable, multi-year framework would preserve and deepen that advantage.
Canada's Exit Raises the Stakes
The pressure on Mexico's track increased sharply when
Canada's negotiations fell apart on August 22
, hours before a midnight deadline for 50% tariffs to take effect on $20 billion in Canadian goods. Prime Minister Mark Carney announced he was suspending talks, saying "last-minute changes in the U.S. proposed terms were unfair, uneconomic, and called into question the reliability of any deal." USTR Greer pushed back, saying Canada had "declined to finalize the trade deal under the terms agreed earlier this week."
Regardless of where the blame lands, the result is that Mexico now negotiates with the full awareness that it stands alone. A failure here does not just hurt Mexico — it effectively ends any functional trilateral framework under the USMCA, at least for the foreseeable future, and leaves the agreement operating in an annual review limbo that serves no one's long-term interests.
What Latino Workers Are Actually Risking
The USMCA's reach into Latino economic life is not abstract.
Over 80 percent of Mexican exports
to the United States qualify under USMCA rules and currently enter duty-free, sustaining a cross-border manufacturing ecosystem that employs hundreds of thousands of Latino workers in auto assembly, steel processing, and logistics. A tariff escalation on vehicles would compress margins for automakers already navigating supply-chain volatility — affecting line workers in Michigan and Ohio as directly as those in Guanajuato and Coahuila.
The
CSIS analysis
of what an interim U.S.-Mexico deal would involve points to the same core dynamic: rolling annual reviews create enough uncertainty to slow investment decisions, which is why both governments have a structural interest in locking in a longer-term arrangement even if full USMCA renegotiation takes years. Any agreement that removes the annual renewal risk gives manufacturers the planning horizon they need to commit to facilities on both sides of the border.
The Remittance Line — and What the Peso Has to Do With It
Remittances to Mexico increased 3.1% in the first six months of 2026, with Mexico
receiving $30.76 billion
between January and June — the second highest first-half total on record, bouncing back from the decline registered in 2025 when aggressive U.S. deportation enforcement had dampened outflows. The average individual transfer came in at $422 in June, up nearly 4% from a year earlier, reflecting stronger labor-market participation among settled Mexican migrants.
The real-value story is more complicated.
BBVA Research estimates
that the peso's appreciation has cut the purchasing power of remittances by roughly 8% year-on-year as of mid-2026 — each dollar buys fewer pesos in a market where domestic prices have continued to rise. If peso strength persists and a trade deal proves elusive, that compression could deepen further.
The other lever is the U.S. outbound transfer levy. BBVA
estimates
that Mexican migrants could pay up to $3 billion in remittance-related taxes between 2026 and 2034 under the current 1% federal excise on cash transfers — a pressure that only compounds if tariff escalation squeezes wages in the manufacturing sectors that produce those dollars in the first place.
The trade deal's outcome feeds directly into that dynamic. A signed framework stabilizes peso expectations and keeps U.S. manufacturing jobs — the engine behind remittance flows — on firm footing. With Ebrard describing Milwaukee as the latest step forward and U.S. midterm voters heading to the polls on November 3, both governments understand what the remaining weeks of genuine negotiating runway are worth.
