Prime Minister Mark Carney said one of the main reasons he walked away from a deal with the Trump administration is because Washington demanded terms that would "destroy" big Canadian industries including the auto, steel and aluminum sectors.
He made the comment while facing calls from the Conservatives to reveal the details of the proposed deal that fell apart late Friday night.
"We did make some progress in recent weeks," Carney said on Monday in Lévis, Que. "In the end, that momentum reversed and we could not accept what the U.S. had offered, nor could we give what they asked."
U.S. Vice-President JD Vance offered a different version of events, accusing Canada of putting forward "a bunch of unreasonable last-minute demands" and urging Carney to "stop taking advantage of America."

While the federal government won't publicly reveal the details of the negotiations, Ontario Premier Doug Ford, who has been briefed on the talks, called it a "bad deal" and said he's glad Carney didn't sign.
Flavio Volpe, a member of Canada's advisory committee on this file and president of the Automotive Parts Manufacturers' Association, said after negotiators agreed in spirit to a potential deal, it was up to dozens of Canadian and American officials to write up the exact wording of an agreement.
When that "text started to crystalize," Volpe said, it didn't match what was discussed.
Here's what CBC News has learned happened behind closed doors.
Pickup trucks left out
CBC News has reported that before the deal's deadline, one of Trump's top deputies — U.S. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick —
expressed dissatisfaction with what had been negotiated and pushed for the U.S. to do better.
Volpe said Lutnick has taken a hard line when it comes to Trump's objective to drain Canada of its vehicle production.
Carney said trade talks broke down with the U.S. in part because American negotiators changed the terms in the hours before the deadline to exclude medium- and heavy-duty trucks from tariff relief.
(Chris Young/The Canadian Press)
American negotiators had initially agreed to drop the headline tariff on Canadian autos from 25 to 15 per cent, Volpe said. A source said American auto content would be exempt, reducing that tariff rate by up to half. (The sector had also been pushing for an exemption for Canadian-made auto content, which the U.S. didn't agree to, a source said.)
Then right before the deadline, the U.S. revealed it planned to exclude medium- and heavy-duty trucks. That move left out North America's most lucrative vehicles: pickup trucks.
Ford's F-Series pickups and the General Motors Silverado are made at Ontario plants and roughly 20,000 workers would have been severely impacted, Volpe said.
"The tariff was going to be so high in those places to not make that product viable," Volpe told
CBC's
The Current
on Monday.
Carney called that exclusion a "big change" to the terms.
Sovereignty threats
In the final hours of talks, the Americans also tried to restrict Canada's ability to strike trade deals with other countries, Carney said.

The U.S. also wanted Canada to apply sectoral tariffs, primarily on steel and aluminum, to its trading partners. Countries that have a free trade deal with Canada would face tariffs on imports above 2025 levels, a source said.
Carney said he was only prepared to align with the U.S. on sectoral tariffs if they benefited Canada.
"That's us deciding to do something that's in our joint interests as opposed to being told to do something or being restricted to doing something else," he said.
Steel, aluminum, lumber
In the end, the prime minister also said there "wasn't enough movement" on American tariff relief for steel, aluminum and derivative products.
Sources said the U.S. had planned to reduce its 50 per cent tariffs on Canadian steel and aluminum to 25 per cent. Derivative products would have been subject to tariff rate quotas, a source said.

Ontario Premier Doug Ford said he heard feedback from the province's steel and auto sectors over the past few days that the U.S. government had put forth a bad trade deal and gave Prime Minister Mark Carney credit for standing up for Canada in a 'very complicated' situation.
Washington was also talking about dropping a 10 per cent Section 232 tariff on Canadian softwood lumber by Sept. 1, but existing countervailing and anti-dumping duties would remain, industry sources said.
Culture
Carney said the Americans also pushed against the Canadian requirements for bilingual product labelling.
U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer dismissed the labelling claim on CNBC, calling it a "funny, fake story."
Carney fired back on Monday saying it's a fact.

He called the labelling issue an American irritant and said in the final hours of negotiations, the Americans took issue with Canadian rules that ensure French-language content is easy to find on streaming platforms like Netflix.
LeBlanc said it was the "cumulative total of a series of things" that surfaced near the end of talks that made Carney decide the agreement in front of them wasn't in the best interest of Canada.
Concessions Canada was willing to make
If the U.S. had agreed to "substantially" lower the tariffs hitting Canadian industries hard, Carney said he was willing to:
Drop Canada's retaliatory tariffs on American steel, aluminum and autos.
Encourage provinces and territories to return American alcohol to the shelves.
Carney asked premiers to end procurement policies that exclude U.S. companies from bidding on projects as a retaliatory measure against American tariffs, Nova Scotia Premier Tim Houston said.
Ottawa was also prepared to adjust dairy import licenses, giving licenses directly to retailers, an industry source said. The move could have made it easier to sell U.S. milk and cheese in Canadian stores, helping the Americans fill their entire duty-free trade quota under CUSMA.

Asked about the criticism, LeBlanc said Carney has been clear that the American tariffs are a violation of the CUSMA agreement Trump signed himself. But he said it wasn't "realistic in those sectors to get to zero tariffs in the short term" because the U.S. is tariffing all of its trading partners.
