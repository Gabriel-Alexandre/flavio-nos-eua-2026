Prime Minister
Mark Carney
has reported a “slight intensification” of talks with the United States over an unfinished
trade
deal that could provide Canada with relief from U.S.
tariffs
, adding any progress that’s been made is because Washington is moving closer to Ottawa’s position.
He did not say how close the two sides are to an agreement that could cut U.S. tariffs on goods such as autos or steel.
The Canadian leader said the chances of a deal with Washington will improve to the extent the U.S. is willing to back off of “red lines” drawn by Ottawa in August.
Ottawa and Washington have ramped up talks for a deal to obtain relief from U.S. tariffs as the White House seeks some kind of breakthrough ahead of midterm elections, six sources familiar with negotiations say. U.S. President Donald Trump is eager to demonstrate to American voters that he is bringing down the cost of living. The Globe is not naming the sources because they were not authorized to speak publicly on the matter.
The Prime Minister also signalled he’s not sure he can trust the U.S. government to honour a future trade deal.
U.S. tariff changes put pressure on new industries
In August, when Canada walked away from trade talks with Washington, Mr. Carney said the U.S. government’s “signature was written in pencil,” meaning it could not be relied upon to abide by agreements.
Asked Monday whether he believes the U.S. government’s signature on a new trade deal would be “in pen,” meaning permanent, Mr. Carney replied: “I don’t know that.”
Talks to resolve a trade war sparked in 2025 by Mr. Trump fell apart this August. Mr. Carney said at the time the U.S. was offering a “bad deal” that “asked too much and offered too little.” In August, he also said U.S. trade proposals would have curtailed Canadian sovereignty and eroded or destroyed major industries here.
Mr. Carney has given three main reasons for abandoning trade talks in August. He said the U.S. refused to extend tariff relief to medium- and heavy-duty trucks, which would have affected the viability of General Motors and Ford plants in Ontario.
He also said the U.S. was pushing for changes around the discoverability of French-language content for online streaming services, and that Washington had suggested language that Ottawa believed could have restricted Canada’s ability to make trade deals with other countries.
U.S. officials have disputed Ottawa’s characterization of the last two points.
In some U.S. states, Trump’s trade war has made Canada an election issue
On Monday, Mr. Carney said Canada wants a better deal than what was offered in the summer.
“Canada is ready to sign an agreement that is in the interests of all Canadians, with very clear bottom lines, red lines, which meant that we were not willing to sign what was offered in August,” the Prime Minister said.
“To the extent any progress has been made, it’s because the U.S. is coming closer to my position,” he said.
Canadian Chamber of Commerce chief executive officer Candace Laing, a member of Ottawa’s advisory committee on Canada-U.S. economic relations, said she was briefed by the Canadian trade team last Thursday.
“We definitely were told it’s a possibility – not necessarily high likelihood – but there’s a possibility that a deal could be reached by Nov. 3,” she said.

The head of Canada’s auto parts lobby group said there may be a window of opportunity to sign a deal.
“I understand the two sides are talking and the President has shown flexibility for strategic midterm interests, so maybe this is a fertile opening,” said Flavio Volpe, president of the Automotive Parts Manufacturers’ Association. “We will wait and see.”
One Canadian official played down current talks with Washington and the chances of a deal before the Nov. 3 midterms, saying no documents have been exchanged and no representatives sent to the U.S. capital.
Last week, Mr. Trump exempted a number of Canadian goods from the 50-per-cent tariffs he imposed in August in order to help Maine Republican Senator Susan Collins’s re-election chances. These included Canadian paperboard cartons, plastic crates, plastic bags, refrigeration equipment and agricultural sorting machinery, among other products.
The White House said it would replace the exempted products with levies on other Canadian imports to maintain the same total amount of Canadian-made goods subject to border taxes.
Prime Minister Mark Carney on Monday said there had been 'a slight intensification' in talks with the United States, adding that Ottawa had not changed its stance.
Reuters
“I think what the President did with Susan Collins was instructional,” Mr. Volpe said. “It says the White House is willing to be flexible in this last week presumably for a more favourable result in the midterms.”
While Republicans appear on track to lose control of the House of Representatives, Mr. Trump appears to believe they might be able to retain control of the Senate.
“A lot of those Senate seats that are in play are in states that are very dependent on the Canada-U.S. relationship,” Mr. Volpe said.
Mr. Trump told a rally in Port Huron, Mich., on Sunday that Canada had been “ripping off” the U.S. on trade and it had no choice but to impose levies on Canadian goods. He said the trade dispute would be settled “pretty soon” and that Canada was “willing to give us anything at this moment.”
A spokesperson for Canada-U.S. Trade Minister Dominic LeBlanc’s office said the two sides “remain in constructive dialogue” on a “range of priorities.” The spokesperson said Canada is ready to work constructively on a mutually beneficial deal that “respects Canada’s interests.”

Several industry sources with knowledge of the talks said they appear to be narrowly focused on the Section 232 sectoral tariffs on automobiles, steel and aluminum.
Two U.S. industry sources said Ottawa and Washington appear to have reached a landing zone for auto tariffs that would be acceptable to both sides and have agreed on certain digital trade issues. One of these sources also said that the U.S. plan is to have an auto deal with Mexico that mirrors the one with Canada.
A Canadian industry source said that Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, whose department is responsible for the Sec. 232 sectoral tariffs, is leading the U.S. side of the talks. During negotiations in August, Mr. Lutnick took a hard line against tariff relief for Canadian metals.
One of the U.S. sources said the Office of the United States Trade Representative is also involved. They said a deal could happen quickly if there’s political will, as most of the major issues were settled in August, and only a handful of items were left to nail down when Canada walked away.
But there was also skepticism about the possibility of a deal in the near term. Another U.S. source said USTR has given few indications that serious negotiations are taking place. For Canada to accept a deal, the U.S. would need to offer meaningful relief on auto and metal tariffs, and that’s unlikely to happen only a week before the election.
One Canadian government source said Canadian trade officials were in Washington last week. But they said that’s not necessarily surprising, given that discussions between the two sides have continued informally since negotiations collapsed in August.
