Caracas, Venezuela
—
After more than 10 months in exile, Venezuelan opposition leader and Nobel laureate
María Corina Machado
has returned home to a political landscape that has been reshaped by her ally the United States without her involvement.
“In Venezuela! Together! For freedom!” she posted on X Sunday night, alongside a video of her greeting a crowd of cheering supporters that surrounded her car, waving Venezuelan flags and blasting joyous music.
Later, she posted she had reached the capital city Caracas – where an equally excited crowd rushed to welcome her and the caravan of vehicles following her journey. People crammed onto overpasses and highways hoping to catch a glimpse of her while a sea of phone cameras flashed in the night.
“It was truly a source of hope for me, for my children, for my grandchildren, and for all Venezuelans,” one supporter in Caracas told CNN. “Just as María Corina returned to Venezuela, my children will return home too.”
Reuters
The Nobel Prize laureate rose to prominence in recent years for her dogged fight for democracy in Venezuela, facing down now-ousted former dictator Nicolás Maduro. She has mostly stayed in hiding after Venezuela’s disputed 2024 presidential election, after which government-controlled electoral authorities declared Maduro the winner – allowing him to cling to power until
his capture
by US special forces in a remarkable raid in January.
Machado’s return from exile raises questions about the uncertain future of Venezuela’s government as it works to transition into a democracy through US-brokered negotiations – that so far have excluded her.
US President Donald Trump said he didn’t know about her return but that he hoped she was safe.
“I don’t know anything about it, but I think she’s a very nice woman,” Trump said when CNN’s Julia Benbrook asked about Machado’s whereabouts. “She gave me her Nobel Prize, so how can I say anything else? And I hope she’s safe.”
Machado
left Venezuela
in December for Norway to accept the Nobel Peace Prize, which she was awarded for promoting democratic rights in her country.

A month after she left, the US toppled
Maduro
and began working with the acting Venezuelan government
on its democratic transition and political reconciliation,
a process that involved several other high-profile
opposition figures
.
Dinorah Figuera, who has been leading the opposition bloc’s negotiations with the government, said Machado’s return – after she was repeatedly refused entry back into Venezuela
–
was a result of Maduro’s ouster and the ongoing talks.
US Secretary of State Marco Rubio echoed those remarks and added: “The United States expects the freedoms of all sectors of Venezuelan politics to continue to be respected. Likewise, we expect all political actors to support these talks which represent the best chance Venezuela has had in thirty years to peacefully achieve not just a free and fair election but an enduring democracy.”
Machado’s return to Venezuela aims to foster a transition and move the country toward holding elections, Pedro Urruchurtu Noselli, director of international relations for the opposition leader and her party, Vente Venezuela, said in a statement on Sunday.
Acting Venezuelan President Delcy Rodríguez described Machado’s return as a “show” and “spectacle” while speaking to reporters Monday.
She declined to say whether Machado would be involved in reconciliation talks but said she hopes the ongoing dialogue will offer a space for all “voices of good” to be heard.
Without naming Machado, she also said she hopes that “those who have harmed Venezuela, those who called for sanctions and external aggression, feel some remorse and regret.” The Venezuelan government has in the past criticized Machado for urging the US to take action against the Maduro regime, including sanctions.
Her journey back to Venezuela
Machado had repeatedly vowed to return home and hinted that she would run for president again if an election were held.
In late August, a key member of her inner circle,
Omar González,
returned to the country after almost three years in exile, in what he called a “prelude” to Machado’s own arrival.
Machado had attempted to re-enter Venezuela
seven times
over the past few months without success, according to her advisers.
She first tried in late June to help with reconstruction efforts after
two powerful earthquakes
devastated the country, but claimed the government thwarted her return by closing its airspace.
She tried again in late September while
Rodríguez
was in New York for the United Nations General Assembly, but she was denied entry three times in a single day.
Rubio said on Monday the US had spoken with the interim Venezuelan authorities about Machado and claimed that any “impediments” to her return “had nothing to do with us.” He noted that the only time he “personally” had discouraged her from returning to Venezuela was “right after the earthquake” because “the only thing we were focused on from a logistical standpoint was getting as many planes with relief aid as possible into the country.”
Rubio said on September 1 that Washington had concerns about her possible return but that she did not need the United States’ approval.
“María Corina Machado, a Venezuelan citizen, has every right to enter her country whenever she wants to return. We have given her advice. For example, during the earthquake, we had many concerns and will continue to have concerns in the future, but we want her safety to be guaranteed,” he said at the time.
While away from home for more than 10 months, Machado traveled the world to rally political support from her allies, including Trump, to whom she gifted her Nobel Prize.

Strained relationship with Trump
While the Trump administration has shown support for Machado in the past, it has largely sidelined her from Venezuela’s ongoing political transition.
After ousting
Maduro
in January, Trump called Machado a “very nice woman,” but claimed she “does not have the support or the respect within the country” to be leader.
Instead, the US has been working with Maduro’s successor
,
Rodríguez, to enact political and economic reforms in the country. Rodríguez has met just about every demand from the Trump administration, including releasing hundreds of political prisoners and making it easier for US companies to invest in the country.
In late August, Trump announced that the US had reached
an agreement
to take majority control of more than 65 billion barrels of proven oil reserves in Venezuela, a move some consider
beneficial
for both the US oil industry and the Venezuelan economy. Machado in a
video statement questioned
why the oil agreement announced by Trump was being signed by the Venezuelan government, which she called an “illegitimate regime.”
The government clung to power in 2024 after state-controlled electoral authorities declared Maduro the winner of the last presidential election, despite experts concluding that such a result was statistically
improbable
.
Rodríguez vowed at the UN General Assembly last month that Venezuela would hold new elections and transition to a full democracy, which the US says is the main goal, but the acting government has yet to announce when a vote will take place.
The National Assembly, the unicameral legislature of Venezuela that is currently controlled by pro-government loyalists, said in a post on X that “the restoration of political guarantees must allow all exiles to return to our country” and that current talks will continue to yield “concrete results” toward recuperating democracy.
Trump has repeatedly said the country is not ready for elections yet but has insisted that a lot of progress has been made.
Asked about whether interim leader Rodríguez can be trusted on a democratic transition, given some of her rhetoric around “external aggression,” Rubio said on Monday, “we ultimately judge it not by rhetoric but by action.”
CNN’s Logan Schiciano, Julia Benbrook and Jennifer Hansler contributed to this report.
